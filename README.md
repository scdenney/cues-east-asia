# Cues of Commitment: Integration and Naturalization Support in South Korea and Taiwan

This repository hosts materials for the working paper *“Cues of Commitment: Integration and Naturalization Support in South Korea and Taiwan.”*

## Authors
- **Steven Denney** (Leiden University)  
- **Nicholas A. R. Fraser** (Toronto Metropolitan University)  
- **H. Christoph Steinhardt** (University of Vienna)  

## Project Overview
This paper examines whether civic fairness, understood as the principle that immigrants who demonstrate civic commitment through participation and engagement are more positively evaluated, extends to support for naturalization in non-Western democracies with strong ethno-nationalist legacies.

We fielded population-representative conjoint survey experiments in both countries (N=2,005 in South Korea; N=2,014 in Taiwan, 2024), complemented by open-text responses for both samples and migrant interview evidence in South Korea. The study advances comparative research on immigrant incorporation by extending theories of civic fairness and intergroup relations beyond Western contexts.

**Keywords**: immigration, naturalization, civic fairness, comparative politics, survey experiments, East Asia  

## Contributions
The paper makes four main contributions:  
1. Demonstrates that social integration cues (e.g., volunteering, civic participation) systematically increase support for naturalization in South Korea and Taiwan.  
2. Shows that responsiveness is moderated by cultural distance: civic engagement neutralizes ethnic and religious penalties in Taiwan, but not fully in South Korea.  
3. Extends cue effects beyond self-identified civic nationalists, indicating a broad democratic logic of inclusion grounded in visible norm adherence.  
4. Provides a comparative test of the civic legacy hypothesis, finding stronger cue effects in Taiwan despite expectations of greater responsiveness in South Korea.  

## Methodology
- **Design**: Choice-based conjoint survey experiments with randomized immigrant profiles across 11 attributes (e.g., civic participation, ethnicity, religion).  
- **Analysis**: Estimation of average marginal component effects (AMCEs) and marginal means, with subgroup and interaction tests.  
- **Supplementary evidence**:  
  - **Qualitative migrant interviews** in South Korea, assessing whether immigrants themselves view civic participation as a pathway to recognition and belonging.  
  - **Open-text topic modeling (LDA)** of manipulation check responses in both countries, confirming that respondents noticed and interpreted integration cues as intended.  

## Key Findings
- **Integration cues matter**: Naturalization support rises by 11–19 percentage points for civically engaged immigrants.  
- **Cultural distance shapes outcomes**: In Taiwan, civic participation offsets ethnic and religious penalties; in South Korea, residual bias persists, especially toward Muslims.  
- **Identity orientations**: Civic nationalists are more sensitive to cues, but effects extend across identity types.  
- **Civic legacies**: Contrary to expectations, cue effects are larger in Taiwan, suggesting that institutional density does not automatically amplify public responsiveness.
